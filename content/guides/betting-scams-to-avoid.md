The most common betting scams to avoid are paid "fixed match" tips, VIP booking codes sold as sure wins, fake mobile money numbers, unlicensed betting sites and fake apps that steal your money or login details. They all rely on the same promise: guaranteed profit with no risk. This guide explains how each scam works, how to check whether a bookmaker is licensed, and what to do if you have been caught out.

## The golden rule

**No one can guarantee a betting win.** Sport is unpredictable, and anyone who claims otherwise is either mistaken or trying to take your money. Genuine tipsters share opinions, not certainties, and genuine bookmakers never ask you to pay a third party to unlock winnings.

## Common betting scams to avoid

### 1. Fixed-match sellers

These scammers claim to have inside information about matches whose results have been arranged in advance. They advertise on Telegram, WhatsApp, Facebook and X, often with screenshots of huge winning slips, and ask for payment before revealing the "fixed" game.

Why it is a scam:

- Genuine match-fixing is a serious crime investigated by sports bodies and police. People involved do not sell the information to strangers online.
- Winning slips are easily faked or edited, or chosen from many losing ones.
- After you pay, the "fixed" match loses, and the seller asks for more money for a "guaranteed" next game or simply blocks you.

### 2. Fake or paid "VIP" booking codes

Booking codes are a normal, free feature that lets people share betslips. Read [what a booking code is](/guides/what-is-a-booking-code) for the basics. Scammers exploit them by selling "VIP" or "sure" codes, sometimes for large sums, claiming insider knowledge.

Warning signs:

- Payment is required before you see the code, often by mobile money or crypto.
- Claims of 100% accuracy or "zero risk".
- Massive odds, such as 50 or 500, presented as near-certain.
- Pressure to pay quickly because "slots are limited".

A booking code is just a list of picks. Paying for one does not make those picks more likely to win. If you see a code you want to try, you can load it or convert it for free with our [booking code converter](/booking-code-converter) and decide for yourself, with a stake you can afford to lose.

### 3. Fake Paybill, till and MoMo numbers

In markets where mobile money is popular, fraudsters post fake payment numbers pretending to belong to well-known bookmakers. Money sent there goes straight to the scammer. Variations include:

- Messages claiming you have won a bonus and must "pay a fee" to release it
- Fake customer care agents asking you to send money to "reverse" a transaction
- Requests for your PIN or one-time password

Only use payment details shown in the bookmaker's official app or website, check the business name on the confirmation screen before entering your PIN, and never share your PIN. See our [mobile money betting guide](/guides/mobile-money-betting-guide) for safe deposit steps.

### 4. Unlicensed and offshore betting sites

Some sites look professional but hold no licence in your country, or claim licences they do not have. They may accept deposits easily, then delay or refuse withdrawals, change terms without notice or simply disappear. Even unlicensed sites that do pay offer no protection if a dispute arises.

### 5. Fake betting apps

Scammers copy the branding of real bookmakers and distribute fake apps through links on social media, messaging apps or unofficial download sites. These apps may steal login details, card data or mobile money PINs.

To stay safe:

- Download apps only from official app stores or the link on the bookmaker's own website.
- Check the developer name and reviews.
- Be suspicious of any app that asks for unusual permissions, such as access to your SMS messages, without a clear reason.

### 6. Account-sharing and "betting investment" schemes

Some schemes ask you to hand over your account login or deposit money with a "professional" who will bet on your behalf for guaranteed returns. These almost always end in losses, stolen funds or a closed account, because sharing accounts breaks bookmaker terms.

## How to check a bookmaker's licence

1. **Look at the website footer.** Licensed bookmakers display their regulator and licence details.
2. **Verify with the regulator.** Search the regulator's public register for the operator's name. Examples include provincial gambling boards in South Africa, the Betting Control and Licensing Board in Kenya, the Gaming Commission of Ghana, the National Lotteries and Gaming Regulatory Board in Uganda, the Gaming Board of Tanzania, state-level authorities in Nigeria such as the Lagos State Lotteries and Gaming Authority, US state gaming regulators, and the Gambling Commission in the UK.
3. **Check the domain.** Make sure the web address exactly matches the official one. Scam sites often use slight misspellings.
4. **Read independent reviews.** Our [bookmaker reviews](/bookmakers) and country pages, such as [Nigeria](/betting-sites/nigeria), [Kenya](/betting-sites/kenya) and [South Africa](/betting-sites/south-africa), only cover licensed operators. Our methodology page explains how we check.

If you cannot confirm a licence, do not deposit.

## Red flags at a glance

| Red flag | Likely scam |
| --- | --- |
| "Guaranteed" or "100% sure" wins | Fixed-match or VIP code seller |
| Pay before you see the tip | Paid tips scam |
| Send money to unlock a bonus or winnings | Fake customer care or payment scam |
| Asked for your PIN or OTP | Account takeover |
| App download link sent by message | Fake app |
| No verifiable licence | Unlicensed site |
| Pressure to act fast | Almost any scam |

## What to do if you have been scammed

1. **Stop sending money.** Scammers often ask for more to "recover" your funds. Recovery-service offers are frequently a second scam.
2. **Contact your bank or mobile money provider immediately** through their official channels. The sooner you report, the better the chance of blocking or tracing the transaction.
3. **Change your passwords and PINs**, especially if you entered details into a fake app or site.
4. **Report it to the police** and keep evidence: screenshots, phone numbers, usernames, transaction references.
5. **Report the account or page** on the social media platform or messaging app where you found it.
6. **Tell your bookmaker** if the scam used its name; it may be able to warn other customers or assist with an investigation.

## Bet safely and responsibly

Scammers target people who are chasing losses or hoping for a quick fix to money problems. If you feel pressure to win back money, that is a sign to step away. Betting is only for adults (18+ in most countries, 21+ in most US states and 25 in Uganda), and our [responsible gambling](/responsible-gambling) page lists free, confidential support.

## FAQ

### Are fixed matches real?

Match-fixing does happen, but it is a crime investigated by sports bodies and law enforcement. People involved do not sell results to strangers on social media. Anyone offering fixed matches for a fee is almost certainly running a scam.

### Should I pay for booking codes?

There is no reason to. Booking codes are a free feature, and paying for one does not make its selections more likely to win. Sellers of "VIP" or "sure" codes are a common scam, especially when they ask for payment by mobile money or crypto.

### How do I know if a betting site is legit?

Check the licence details in the site footer and confirm them on the regulator's public register for your country. Make sure the web address is spelled correctly and read independent reviews. If you cannot verify a licence, do not deposit.

### Can I get my money back after sending it to a fake Paybill?

It is difficult but not always impossible. Contact your mobile money provider immediately through its official customer care, report the transaction and the number, and file a police report. Acting quickly gives you the best chance.

### Is a betting app safe if a friend sent me the link?

Not necessarily. Fake apps often spread through shared links. Only download betting apps from official app stores or from the link on the bookmaker's own website, and check the developer name before installing.
